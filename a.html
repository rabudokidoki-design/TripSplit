A · Summary · 1 of 3

Executive summary: background and objectives

In 22 weeks, [Company] moves from no stated baseline controls to a minimum set of controls with evidence, so management can see the remaining risks before the IPO application.

Background
The business
[Company] is a clinical-stage biotech with about 200 staff, based in Hong Kong with offices in Hong Kong and Mainland China.
The trigger
[Company] plans an IPO application within about 6 months. IPO advisers, once appointed, may ask how systems and data are protected.
The starting point
[Company] states it has no baseline cybersecurity controls; [We] verify this in Week 1. Data sits in finance, cloud, staff devices and paper notebooks.
The constraint
The runway is short. Controls need time to run and leave evidence, so the quick start fixes begin in Week 1.
Objectives
Know the gaps
Assess 14 control domains against recognised international references, using interviews and evidence checks.
Plan it
Rank gaps by threat and build a 22-week roadmap sized for about 200 people, with an owner for each fix and three decision meetings (gates).
Close the minimum gaps
Put a starting set of minimum controls in place (about 15). [Company] confirms the final list at the first gate, Week 6.
Evidence it
Keep dated records for every control, so management can see what is in place and what risk remains.
Target at Week 22 — The minimum controls are in place and evidenced, and management has accepted the residual risks. [We] do not claim IPO readiness or compliance.

IPO baseline: reference and what is not claimed

HKEX material on risk management is a reference point for this work, not a pass mark. [We] show what is in place and what is not, and do not claim IPO readiness or compliance.
1 · What the reference says
THE REFERENCE
HKEX’s Corporate Governance Code (Appendix C1 to the Listing Rules) has the board oversee risk management and internal control. HKEX guidance includes cyber risk within that review.
HOW TO READ IT
The Code is written for listed issuers, not IPO applicants. [We] do not read it as a cybersecurity baseline for applicants; it is a reference point only.
NOT A CYBER REFERENCE
Appendix C2 (ESG Reporting Code) is not a cybersecurity reference.
WHY IT STILL MATTERS
A company that lists becomes a listed issuer, so the direction is clear. A baseline built now avoids a rush later.

Main threats and the three tests

Five threats are the starting list for deciding what to fix first. A control joins the minimum set only if it passes three tests. Phase 1 may change the list; the Executive Sponsor decides it at Gate 1.
TS-A
Cloud account taken over (OneDrive, Jianguoyun)
Why it fits: preclinical data sits in two cloud platforms; sign-in controls to be confirmed in Week 1.
TS-B
Fraudulent or wrong payment (one ERP, both offices)
Why it fits: one ERP serves both offices and handles vendor payments; approval steps to be confirmed in Week 1.
TS-C
Lost, stolen or infected device (BYOD)
Why it fits: staff use their own PCs and laptops for work; the standard for them is to be confirmed in Week 1.
TS-D
R&D data lost, copied out or not provable
Why it fits: lab notes are on paper at two sites and datasets sit in a Mainland cloud; leaver and record practice to be confirmed.
TS-E
Data loss or ransomware, with no tested restore
Why it fits: research data sits in cloud platforms; backup and restore arrangements are to be confirmed in Week 1.
Three tests for a minimum control
1
It breaks a threat chain
It stops or slows at least one of the five threats.
2
Evidence can be shown
A screenshot, record or document can prove it runs.
3
[Company] can keep it running
After Week 22, with generalist IT and a part-time coordinator. If it fails, fold it into another control or defer it.
Who decides. The five threats are [We]’s starting proposal, not a NIST or ISO list. Phase 1 may give fewer or more. The Executive Sponsor decides the final list at Gate 1, on [We]’s recommendation.
